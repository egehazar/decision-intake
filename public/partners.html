Quick Benefits CheckDeveloper guide · Source

Help the people you serve find the benefits they're missing

For food banks, libraries, 211 lines, clinics and navigator teams. The Quick Benefits Check asks about 8 questions and says which of fifteen US programs a household should apply for, in English or Spanish. It is free and open source, and it stores nothing.

  • 15 programsSNAP, WIC, Medicaid, CHIP, TANF, SSI, school meals, federal and state tax credits, Head Start, housing
  • About 8 questionsit picks the next question worth asking and stops when another isn't
  • Nothing storedno accounts, no database; answers stay in the visitor's browser tab
  • Says when it can't telland hands off to a person instead of guessing

      2 · For a list of households
      
Rank a caseload

      
Upload a spreadsheet of households you already know a little about (state, household size, children, earnings, rent). Get them back ranked by the benefits each is likely missing, with the one question to ask each first.

      
In a development check on 2,000 simulated households known by five fields, the top fifth of this ranking held 61% of the benefit dollars, against 20% in random order and 53% for largest household first. Use your own reference numbers, never names; rows are scored and not kept.

      3 · In your systems
      
Connect it to your tools

      
A JSON API for intake forms and case management systems, and an MCP connector so AI assistants (Claude, ChatGPT and others) run the screening with the engine choosing the questions instead of guessing.

      
Endpoints for each step, the full assessment, what would change a result, the order to apply in, and a caseload at once. Free; 120 requests a minute from one address.

What to know before you use it

    

      
What it is
A screening estimate from PolicyEngine's open model of the 2026 rules, with fourteen statutory limits it may not break. Not an application and not an eligibility decision: only the program decides, and anyone can apply whatever it says.

      
How it was tested
On thousands of simulated households it had never seen, against the rules model. It has not yet been tested with real applicants. Immigration status isn't asked and can change eligibility.

      
When it can't tell
Where the answers fit households that turn out very differently, it says so and points to a person (211 or a benefits navigator) rather than calling a program unlikely.

      
Privacy
No accounts, no database, no answers in logs. The screener keeps answers in the visitor's browser tab until they clear them or close it.

      
Cost and support
Free. Open source, so you can run your own copy. Questions and problems: GitHub issues.
